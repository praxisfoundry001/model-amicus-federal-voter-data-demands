# Model Brief of Amicus Curiae Praxis Foundry

**In Support of State Election Officials, Voter Plaintiffs, and Voting-Rights Organizations Opposing Unredacted Federal Voter-Data Demands**

**A Voter Roll Is Not a Suspect List**

## What is this?

This is a model amicus brief. It has not been filed in any court and is not legal advice.

It is a public constitutional-structure artifact drafted in amicus form for litigation challenging or resisting federal demands for unredacted statewide voter-registration data, including sensitive personal identifiers.

## Why should I care?

A voter-registration record is not just a database entry.

It is a recognition record. It exists because a state has received a person into its election system as a registered voter. That record helps preserve ballot access, election notices, polling-place assignment, eligibility maintenance, participation history, and election administration.

The danger addressed here is recognition inversion: when voter-recognition records are treated as federal risk files.

## What is the claim?

The core claim is simple:

**A voter roll is not a suspect list.**

Federal inspection authority, where it exists, must remain bounded by statutory purpose and voter-protective safeguards. It should not become permission to centralize, retain, match, share, or repurpose sensitive statewide voter data in a way that reclassifies registered voters as federal risk objects.

## What is this not?

This is not legal advice.

It is not a filed brief, a full election-law treatise, or a claim that voter-roll maintenance is illegitimate. Accurate voter rolls matter. Lawful list maintenance matters.

The brief argues that maintenance is not the same thing as suspicion, and that any federal access to sensitive voter data must preserve the voter’s recognition status.

## How was this done?

The brief translates a live public-law problem into a constitutional-structure argument.

It uses public reporting, public litigation materials, litigation trackers, and DOJ / OLC materials as public-source labels rather than law-review footnotes. It frames the problem through voter recognition, federalism as scale discipline, minimization, use limits, retention and deletion rules, auditability, and voter-facing due process before consequence.
